7 Things About the Silk Road Most Kids Never Learn

Camels, copper coins and traders who spoke many languages. Here is the real story, with something to try after each fact.

  1. Nobody called it the Silk Road at the time

    The name was made up in the 1870s by a German geographer, Ferdinand von Richthofen. The people who travelled the routes just called it “the road”, and it was really a web of many paths, not one road.

    Try it: Ask your child: why might a name invented over 1,000 years later still be useful?

  2. Almost nobody travelled the whole way

    Goods usually passed from trader to trader, like a relay race. A bolt of silk might change hands many times before it reached the far end, and each trader added a little to the price.

    Try it: Pass an object around a circle and have each person “add 1” to its price.

  3. Sogdian merchants were the star traders

    The Sogdians came from cities such as Samarkand, in what is now Uzbekistan. They spoke several languages, set up homes along the routes, and were famous for keeping careful accounts.

    Try it: Find Samarkand on a map and trace the road east to Chang’an.

  4. Tang Chang’an was one of the biggest cities on Earth

    In the 700s, the Tang capital held about a million people inside its walls. Its markets sold horses, spices, glass and silk from many lands.

    Try it: Compare it with your own town or city. How many times bigger was Chang’an?

  5. Money was copper, and sometimes silk

    Tang China used round copper coins with a square hole in the middle, threaded on cords. In some places, bolts of silk were also used like money.

    Try it: Draw a coin with a square hole. Why might a hole help when you carry hundreds?

  6. Camels were the trucks of the desert

    Camels could walk for days with little water. But a camel can only carry so much, and overloading one is a bad bargain, because the goods and the animal both suffer.

    Try it: Pack a bag for a trip. What is the heaviest thing you could leave behind?

  7. Caravans carried more than goods

    Along with silk, glass and spices, travellers carried songs, recipes, religions and ideas. Cities on the road were often mixed places where people of many cultures lived side by side.

    Try it: Which food or word in your home came from somewhere far away?
